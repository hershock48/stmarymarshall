A letter to the Diocese of Kalamazoo

Forty-two parish websites.
One template.
Five gaps on every one.

I opened every parish website in the diocese in a browser and measured it. Then I rebuilt three parishes, one of them in Spanish, on a single platform the diocese would own. They are running today at the links below. This letter says what the measurements found, what the three sites do, and what it would cost to do the rest.

Section one

What we found.

The diocesan Parish Finder lists 60 parishes and missions with 46 distinct websites. I opened each one in a real browser on November 2, 2026, twice, and read its markup, home page only. Two sites did not work at all: sscmparish.org in Wayland fails its security handshake and stedwardmendon.org in Mendon does not resolve. Two more, Bridgman and White Pigeon, were built by hand outside any platform. Of the 42 that run on one, 41 are eCatholic and one is WordPress (St. Joseph, Battle Creek). The diocese’s own site is eCatholic too.

St. Anthony Buchanan’s home page has 85 links with no words on them

Links with no text, no image and no label: a screen reader announces each one as “link” and nothing else, 85 times in a row. St. Ambrose Parchment has 61 of them, San Felipe 28. They are the template’s slideshow and tab controls, which is why the count is high wherever a parish uses either. Nobody at the parish did this; nobody at the parish can undo it.

The one asset the template has feeds the diocese’s own finder, and 12 parishes do not use it

eCatholic’s MassTime panel is what lets the diocesan Parish Finder show a Mass schedule. 30 of the 42 sites use it; 12 type their times into a page as text, so the finder has nothing to read from them. The panel is also the reason a replacement cannot simply be a prettier template: whatever comes next has to publish the schedule as data from day one, or the finder goes dark one parish at a time.

The Parish Finder ↗

Every parish’s photos, PDFs and calendar live on eCatholic’s servers

Registration forms, cemetery maps, pastors’ letters, Religious Education calendars and every slideshow photograph are served from files.ecatholic.com/<account>/…, and every calendar is an eCatholic feed. The day a subscription ends, every one of those links stops working, and no parish holds a copy. I know this because I rebuilt St. Mary Marshall from those files and had to bring each one home.

What the account numbers say

eCatholic numbers its customers in order. Seven parishes in the diocese carry numbers under 15,000, early customers with their own accounts; St. Mary Marshall is 6225. Seventeen sit in one tight block from 19,059 to 19,971, which is a batch signed up together, almost certainly the diocesan partnership eCatholic advertises. The rest trail upward to 39,077. So “the diocese pays for the website” is probably true for the seventeen and probably not for the others, which is a question the diocese can answer in an afternoon and no parish office can.

None of this is an opinion about design. Each line is a measurement, the same on every parish because the template is the same on every parish, and each one is fixed by the build, not by a parish secretary.

Section two

The part that matters most.

Forty-one parishes pay for the same template, directly or through the diocese, and at the end of every month the diocese owns none of it: not the pages, not the photos, not the calendars, not the Mass schedule its own finder depends on.

What leaving looks like, in eCatholic’s own words

Its terms of service say that on termination a parish may copy and paste its content and download the files it uploaded, and that “eCatholic will not provide you with any form of export of your Website or the Platform.” The same document names the sole remedy for any dispute: cancel the subscription. That is the position every parish in the diocese is in today, and the one the diocese would be in on behalf of all of them.

What I would do instead

The diocese owns the platform. The parishes are its tenants.

One codebase, every parish on its own domain with its own photos and words, none of them a template. The diocese holds the repository, the hosting and the data, in writing. The Mass schedule of every parish is published as structured data and as one diocesan feed, so the finder, Google and the Mass-finder apps read it from the diocese instead of from a vendor. If the diocese ever leaves me, it gets the working project, repository, hosting and logins. Not a snapshot. Not an export. A twelve-month first term on the diocesan agreement, then month to month, thirty days’ notice, no exit fee. That clause is titled No hostage-taking, because I would rather say it plainly than bury it.

Three questions worth asking this month

Who holds the eCatholic agreement, and when does it end?If the diocese signed for the block of seventeen, that date decides whether this is a 2027 conversation or a 2028 one. The other parishes can move whenever they like.
Who holds the 46 domain logins?Two sites are down today for want of a renewal or a certificate. A domain is the most valuable piece of the internet a parish has, and most of them are in one person’s inbox.
What does the Parish Finder read, exactly?The feed format it consumes. The three sites below already publish a feed; matching the finder’s shape is a route, not a project, once the format is in hand.

How the rest would be built

The 41 eCatholic sites share one markup, which is why St. Philip’s 78 pages took a day: the tabs, the MassTime panel, the staff list, the documents on files.ecatholic.com and the calendar feed are all in predictable places. Each parish is read into the same data shape the three sites use, a person reads the result once, and the parish office confirms the handful of facts only it knows: holy day Mass times, office hours, which inbox the forms go to. The order: the three sites here, then the parishes the diocese picks, then the block of seventeen when their agreement allows, then the fourteen parishes and missions with no website at all.

Two of the three sites were built from public pages without the parishes’ involvement, as a demonstration for the diocese. They are hidden from search engines and would not go live at a parish’s address without that parish’s yes and its office’s corrections. St. Mary’s was built with the parish’s permission.

The struck number is Glazed Web’s own published price for a six-page site with forms, the Baker’s Dozen on glazedweb.com ↗. The monthly is the menu’s smallest, the one that goes with a one-page site, applied to every parish whatever its size.

For the 42 parishes with a working site today that is $4,158 a month, for a platform the diocese owns outright. eCatholic’s own entry plan is $99 a month per site. The diocesan agreement runs twelve months the first year, which is what stands in for a build fee; after that every parish is month to month. A parish that comes on its own, outside the agreement, pays the published build price. Work beyond keeping the sites current, a diocesan directory, a Mass finder of the diocese’s own, an office panel for self-service edits, is quoted first and approved before it starts.

Why the monthly is not lower

A website with nobody paid to mind it stops being minded; twelve sites with no date on them and one stuck in 2022 say so. The monthly is what makes “can you change the Christmas Mass times” a normal request to a vendor rather than a favor, and it is the same arrangement every other client of mine has, in writing.

Section five

What happens next.

1

Open the three sites

Ten minutes each, on a phone. St. Mary, St. Philip, San Felipe. Find the next Mass. Subscribe to a calendar. Send a prayer request and watch it land.

2

One call

Thirty minutes with whoever holds the eCatholic agreement and the Parish Finder. I bring the full table, one row per site. You bring the agreement date and the finder’s feed format.

3

Pick the next parishes

The diocese names the parishes it wants moved first. Each one is a day of reading plus the office’s short list of facts, and goes live at its own address when its office says so.

4

The finder reads from the diocese

The feed takes the finder’s shape, and from then on the diocese is the source of its own schedule data. The eCatholic subscriptions end parish by parish, after each site is live, so nothing is ever offline.
