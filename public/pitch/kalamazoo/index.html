A letter to the Diocese of Kalamazoo

Sixty parishes.
One living platform.
Connected to each other, and to the diocese.

This began as one parish website. One of us sits in the pews at St. Mary in Marshall, and the site was bad enough that we rebuilt it on our own time. When we opened the other parish sites in the diocese we found the same rented template on nearly every one, each parish on its own island, nothing shared between them and nothing for the diocese to see. So we stopped building a website and built a platform. Parishes connected to each other through one finder, one calendar and one set of groups. A layer for the diocese to see every site at once, put the bishop’s voice on all of them the hour he speaks, and send a notice everywhere in one line. And under both, a parish site that is alive, where the schedule, the calendar and the week’s news run from the same data and the office types nothing twice. The faith is living and colorful; the websites should be too. The finder covers all sixty parishes today, and three parishes run on the platform.

Section one

Parishes connected to each other.

Every parish keeps its own site, and the finder, the diocesan calendar, the groups and the nearby strip read from what the parishes already keep. Connection costs no parish a minute: the coffee hour on one calendar is on the diocese’s page the same hour, and a Catholic in a car finds the next Mass in the next town without knowing the parish’s name. The finder covers all sixty today, the three on the platform from their own data and the rest read from the diocese’s own page, and it gets better one parish at a time.

The diocese layer

Mass near you, for all sixty parishes, from the first day

A zip code, a town, or the phone’s own location, and every parish within ten, twenty-five or fifty miles, nearest first, with its next Mass worked out from the clock. It covers all sixty parishes and missions today: the three on the platform from their own data, the other fifty-seven read from the diocese’s own public Parish Finder, each marked with where its times came from. Under the agreement that reading is done at the diocese’s request, of the diocese’s own page, and the diocese is the source of the result. The diocese’s finder stops depending on eCatholic the week this goes live, before a single parish moves, and it gets better one parish at a time as each one joins. Every parish site carries the three nearest parishes and their next Mass at the foot of its own Mass times page.

Take the finder today, with nothing to sign. It runs on its own address, re-reads the diocese’s Parish Finder every Monday, says on every card when it last did, and carries a “wrong time? tell us” link that reaches the chancery. Point the Parish Finder page on diokzoo.org at it, or embed it there (it is built to allow that for diokzoo.org and no other site), and the diocese has a finder of its own from that day: free for ninety days, and under the license for as long as it runs, whether or not a single parish ever moves.

The diocese layer

This week across the diocese, and the groups

Every event on every parish calendar on the platform on one page, filtered by kind and town: the coffee hour, the Bible study, the fish fry, the young adult night two towns over, each with an add-to-calendar link. Beside it, every council, ministry and study group on every parish, with the person to call, so someone in one town finds the group in the next. Nobody posts anything; the parish calendar is the post. A notice from the chancery (the appeal, a retreat, a safe-environment reminder) is written once and appears on every parish site that has not turned it off, which is the reverse of what eCatholic’s Diocesan Sharing does, in a file the diocese holds.

Section two

What the diocese sees, and what it sends.

A diocese cannot coordinate what it cannot see, and today it sees sixty separate sites through sixty separate logins. The platform gives the chancery one place to look and one place to speak. What it sends: the bishop’s voice, long form and short, a homily or a Lent reflection on every parish home page the hour it is posted, with a feed that does not depend on a social network; a notice written once that lands on every site; and this week across the diocese, the gatherings, the parish missions, the young adult night two towns over, compiled into one page that is also the weekly email. What it sees: every site’s health and use on one page, by deanery, with counts and no person in them. This is the part no template offers, because a template was never built to be a diocese.

For the communications office

None of this asks the communications office to change a tool. The parish calendars become the submissions for the Tuesday email: this week across the diocese is one page and one feed, which the office trims and sends from the mail program it already uses. A notice is typed once, with the dates it should run, and is on every parish site that minute. A recording of the bishop is listed once. diokzoo.org stays as it is, and the finder can sit inside it.

The diocese layer

The bishop’s voice on every parish site, and Watch

What Bishop Lohse has said to the diocese, in his own voice: the Easter and Christmas messages in English and Spanish, the Chrism Mass homily, his talk to parish staff. The diocese lists a recording once and every parish home page carries the newest one, in the parish’s language where it exists, with a feed anyone can subscribe to in a podcast app, so none of it depends on Facebook. Each parish that streams gets a Watch page that knows the schedule: the player from a quarter hour before Mass to ninety minutes after, the next Mass in words the rest of the week, so nobody meets an offline player. Today it plays from the parish’s own YouTube or Facebook; hosting the stream ourselves, with automatic recordings, clips and the bishop’s Mass shared to every site at once, is the one priced add-on in this letter, $89 a month for a parish that streams. The same data gives every parish a line at the top of every page in the week before a holy day, with the Mass times, which is the one thing the St. Mary office told us it liked about the old site.

Section three

Under the finder and the chancery’s view sits what each parish gets. Three parishes run on the platform today: St. Mary Marshall, St. Philip Battle Creek and San Felipe de Jesús in Fennville, in Spanish throughout. The schedule, the calendar, the holy days, the news, the forms and the finder all read from one set of data the office keeps by sending a text, so the site is right on the day a Mass time changes and the week before a holy day, and it stays right when the secretary retires. Written once

The week’s news, the bulletin insert and the diocesan email, from the same rows

A parish office today types the same announcement into the bulletin, the website, Flocknote and Facebook, and the diocese’s communications office assembles its Tuesday email from whatever parishes remembered to forward by Thursday. Why it matters

The website is the front door now.

Not for everyone, and not instead of the person in the pew who invites a neighbor. But the research is consistent on three points: people who are looking for a parish look online more every year, the ones who do judge the parish by what they see, and Catholics themselves now use the parish website in numbers nobody predicted.

What we would do instead

The diocese owns everything on it, and everything about it.

Glazed Web builds the platform and keeps it running. One codebase, every parish on its own domain with its own photos and words, none of them a template. The diocese owns everything it puts in and everything about it, in writing: every page, photo and document, every schedule, calendar, event and form, its domains in its own registrar account, its giving on each parish’s own Stripe account with every donor and recurring gift in it. The Mass schedule of every parish is published as structured data and as one diocesan feed, so the finder, Google and the Mass-finder apps read it from the diocese instead of from a vendor. Export at any time, free, as readable files, as data and as a copy of each site that runs on any host; feeds nobody is charged for. And if we ever fail the diocese, if we close, stop the platform or stop answering, it receives a perpetual license to the software as it stands that day, source included, to run for its own parishes, with a sealed copy already delivered to it every quarter. Nothing of the diocese’s lives only on our server. A twelve-month first term on the diocesan agreement, then month to month, thirty days’ notice, no exit fee. That clause is titled No hostage-taking, because we would rather say it plainly than bury it.

The diocese next door

Nothing obliges a Michigan diocese to keep its parishes on one rented template. Lansing’s trouble is the opposite of Kalamazoo’s, since its parishes share nothing at all, and one platform answers both.

A parish that comes on its own, outside the license, pays the published build price and $99 a month.

Against what is paid today

We do not know what the diocese pays eCatholic, so here are the published numbers. Where a diocese bills its parishes centrally the rate is $50 a site a month, and the list price starts at $99. For the forty-one parishes on it that is $24,600 to $48,700 a year for the template, with every edit still done by someone in a parish office. The license is $47,880. Against the lower figure that is about $23,000 a year more, and the difference pays for nineteen more parishes and missions, fourteen of which have no website today, for every edit at every parish done by us the same week, and for the finder, the bishop’s voice and the weekly digest, which no site fee includes at either price.

For the finance office

One invoice a month to the diocese, $3,990, beginning the month after the first parish site is live on its own domain. Nothing is billed before that and nothing is ever billed to a parish. There is no build fee, no setup fee and no exit fee. The first term is twelve months; after it either side ends the agreement on thirty days’ notice. Giving, stated plainly

A parish that takes gifts through the platform does so on its own Stripe account at the nonprofit rate, 2.2% plus 30 cents a gift, and the platform’s fee is 1% of the gift, capped at $5, which the donor is offered to cover and most do. Nothing is due monthly for giving; a parish that gives little pays little, and a $5,000 memorial gift pays $5. The parish’s donors and their recurring gifts live on the parish’s own account and travel with it. The Annual Appeal can run on the same rails, on the diocese’s own account, with every parish’s progress on the chancery view. A parish that keeps Vanco or OSV keeps it; the Give button goes straight there.

What a parish may add, and pays for itself

Hosting its livestream on its own player with automatic recordings and clips, $89 a month. An office panel for self-service edits, $29 a month. Texting at cost plus a margin. Each one a parish chooses; none is required, and the license does not change when one is added.

Who keeps a parish website today

The people who run parish life are in their fifties and sixties. That is the Church’s own count: CARA puts the median lay ecclesial minister at 55 with most of them over 50, and parish life coordinators at 57 to 60, on a staff of under ten where the website is nobody’s job. So it falls to the secretary or to a parishioner who learned eCatholic’s page builder on their own time and holds the only password. There is nothing wrong with that person; they are who answers the phone at a parish. The problem is what the page builder asks of them. They have to learn a piece of software, keep up with it as it changes, and spend office hours inside it every week. When they retire or step back, the next person, usually older still, starts over from the training videos, or the site simply stops. Those are hours the parish pays for that nobody budgeted, and the sites show the result: a bishop who left in 2023 still on a history page, twelve sites with no date on them at all.

We are younger than the people this is being asked of, and we do this for a living. That is the whole reason the arrangement works. Under the monthly, nobody at any parish logs into anything. The office sends the change by text or email, it is up the same week, and when the secretary retires the website does not notice.

Why the license is one number

A website with nobody paid to mind it stops being minded; twelve sites with no date on them and one stuck in 2022 say so. The license is what makes “can you change the Christmas Mass times” a normal request rather than a favor to ask of a parishioner, at every parish, including the fourteen with no site at all. One number means no parish is ever the one that could not afford to join.

The license

One line, every parish included, and the parishes move at whatever pace their offices want. The eCatholic subscriptions end parish by parish, after each site is live, so nothing is ever offline, and the finder never goes dark, because under the license it runs whether a parish has moved or not.

How the parishes would move

The 41 eCatholic sites share one markup, which is why St. Philip’s 78 pages took a day: the tabs, the MassTime panel, the staff list, the documents on files.ecatholic.com and the calendar feed are all in predictable places. Each parish is read into the same data shape the three sites use, a person reads the result once, and the parish office confirms the handful of facts only it knows: holy day Mass times, office hours, which inbox the forms go to. The order is the diocese’s to set. Ours would be the fourteen parishes and missions with no website first, since they have nothing to move and no agreement to wait out; then the parishes the diocese names; then the rest as each one’s eCatholic agreement allows. We do not know when those agreements end. Seventeen account numbers sit in one block, which suggests one agreement signed at once, and its end date is the first thing we would ask on the call.
