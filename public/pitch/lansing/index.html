A letter to the Diocese of Lansing

Seventy-three parishes.
One living platform.
Connected to each other, and to the diocese.

We build websites for a living, and one of us sits in the pews in the Diocese of Kalamazoo, where a single parish site we rebuilt as a gift turned into a platform for the whole diocese: parish sites that are alive, connected to each other through one finder and one calendar, with a layer for the diocese to see every site and put the bishop’s voice on all of them at once. Parishioners there hold Lansing up as the diocese that gets this right: the bishop’s podcast, the parishes that stream, the diocesan site built by hand rather than rented. So we read every one of Lansing’s 73 parish websites and the diocese’s own directory, and found the one thing a diocese this far ahead still does not have: a way for its parishes to be connected. Seventy-three sites on seven platforms, each on its own island, and a diocesan site that sends people to MassTimes.org to find a Mass. We built the finder for all 73 from the diocese’s own directory, and put the bishop’s podcast in it as his voice. Both are running now.

Section one

What we found.

The diocese’s own parish list names 73 parishes in six vicariates with 72 distinct websites (one parish’s “website” is its Facebook page). We opened each one in a real browser on November 4, 2026, on a phone profile, and read its markup, home page only. Five did not work: stmarymorrice.org refuses the request, christthekingparishflint.org fails its security certificate (the name on the certificate is not the site’s), and srbcatholic.com, stjos.com and the Facebook page did not load inside forty-five seconds.

Twenty-six parishes rent the same template Kalamazoo’s forty-one do

The eCatholic sites in Lansing carry the same five gaps we measured on every eCatholic site in the Diocese of Kalamazoo, because the template is the same: no schedule as data, no link card, a doubled heading on every inner page, and a contract that forbids export of any kind. The other 41 parishes each chose a platform of their own, which is why a chancery cannot hand its parishes a standard it does not have.

Section two

The finder the diocese does not have.

Open dioceseoflansing.org/find-a-parish ↗. It lists the parishes by city, by vicariate and by county, with a map. Ask it when the next Mass is and it cannot say; the directory holds no times, and the site’s own answer is a link to MassTimes.org, a national volunteer directory the diocese does not control and the parishes rarely update. A Catholic in a car in Flint on a Saturday afternoon is on her own.

Every parish has the times, on its own site, in seven different shapes. What is missing is the one place that reads them all, and the only way that place stays right is if the parishes feed it by keeping their own site right. That is what Narthex is: the parish website and the diocesan finder are the same data, typed once.

Running now

Mass near you, for all 73 parishes, from the diocese’s own directory

A zip code, a town, or the phone’s own location, and every parish within ten, twenty-five or fifty miles, nearest first, each with its address, phone, site and vicariate, read from the diocese’s own parish list. Today it carries every parish and no times, because the diocese publishes none; the day a parish joins the platform its Mass, Confession and Adoration times appear on its card, worked out from the clock, and a Catholic in a car gets an answer. It re-reads the directory weekly, says on every card when it last did, carries a “wrong time? tell us” link that reaches the chancery, and can be embedded on the diocese’s own site. Take it today, with nothing to sign.

Running now

The bishop’s voice, on every parish site at once

Bishop Boyea’s Made for Mission podcast is already the first entry: the recording the diocese published, listed once, with a feed anyone can subscribe to in a podcast app, and a card every parish home page on the platform carries. Add a homily, a Lent reflection or a letter read aloud and it is on every site the same hour, in English or Spanish where it exists in both, with no one at any parish copying anything. For the parishes that stream, each gets a Watch page that knows the Mass schedule: the player from a quarter hour before Mass to ninety minutes after, the next Mass in words the rest of the week, so nobody meets an offline player.

Section three

The part that matters most.

Seventy-three parishes pay seven vendors, and at the end of every month the diocese owns none of it: not the pages, not the photos, not the calendars, and not a schedule of its own parishes.

What the diocese would hold

The diocese owns the platform. The parishes are its tenants.

The platform is called Narthex, after the entrance hall of a church where the bulletin board and the sign-up sheets live, and Glazed Web builds it. One codebase, every parish on its own domain with its own photos and words, none of them a template. The diocese holds the repository, the hosting and the data, in writing. Every parish’s schedule is published as structured data and as one diocesan feed, so the finder, Google and the Mass-finder apps read it from the diocese instead of from a vendor. Export at any time, feeds nobody is charged for, giving on the parish’s own account with no percentage to anyone. If the diocese ever leaves us, it gets the working project, repository, hosting and logins. Not a snapshot. Not an export. A twelve-month first term on the diocesan agreement, then month to month, thirty days’ notice, no exit fee.

What stays, what changes

A parish keeps everything that works. The platform replaces the website and takes on the schedule, the calendar and the forms; it links to the rest rather than replacing it.

Section four

What is running.

No Lansing parish has been built yet; that is the first call’s decision, not ours to make for a parish. What is running is the finder and the bishop’s voice for Lansing, and three complete parish sites in the Diocese of Kalamazoo on the same platform, which is what a Lansing parish would get: St. Mary Marshall ↗, St. Philip Battle Creek ↗ (78 pages read in), and San Felipe de Jesús ↗, in Spanish throughout. Each has its Mass times as data, the next Mass from the clock, a calendar anyone can subscribe to, three forms that reach the office, religious education enrollment, the sacraments as checklists, a news page that prints as the bulletin insert, and the diocese layer at its foot.

A first Lansing parish is a day of reading its current site into the platform’s data, a person reading the result once, and the office confirming the handful of facts only it knows. Name three and we build them before the call, at no charge, as the three Kalamazoo sites were built.

The finder and the bishop’s voice are the diocese’s to use today, before any of this.

Who keeps a parish website today

The people who run parish life are in their fifties and sixties; CARA puts the median lay ecclesial minister at 55. The website falls to the secretary or a parishioner who learned one of seven page builders on their own time and holds the only password. We are younger than the people this is being asked of, and we do this for a living. Under the monthly, nobody at any parish logs into anything. The office sends the change by text or email, it is up the same week, and when the secretary retires the website does not notice.
